When 150 DPI is the right choice

150 is not the photo-lab habit. It is the density large-format printers actually specify — coarse enough that a 24 × 36 in poster does not demand an 8K file, fine enough that type and photos still read from the aisle. The reason is viewing distance. At arm’s length the eye can pick out a 300-pixel grid. A few feet back, it cannot. Four jobs show up over and over:

Posters (24 × 36 and similar). A standard 24 × 36 in poster at 150 DPI needs 3600 × 5400 px. The same poster at 300 would demand 7200 × 10800 px — four times the pixels, four times the file, for detail nobody can see from six feet. Poster houses and online large-format shops write “150 DPI, full-bleed, CMYK or RGB as specified” because that is the density their RIP and their paper can hold at that size. A 3600 × 5400 px file tagged 72 claims a 50-inch wall; tagged 150 it claims 24 × 36 in, which is the size the quote reserved. Preflight that checks the integer wants to see 150, not 72 and not 300.

Banners and retractables. Pull-up banners, street-pole flags, and vinyl runs are viewed from several feet, often in motion. 150 is the usual floor; some outdoor shops drop to 72–100 on pieces taller than a person. Sending a 300 tag does not make the vinyl sharper — it just claims a smaller print, or it pads a resample the RIP will throw away. Rewriting 150 keeps the pixels, keeps the bytes, and matches the spec on the work order.

Trade-show graphics. Booth backdrops, hanging signs, and table throws are designed to read from the aisle, not from six inches. Exhibit houses almost always specify 150 DPI at the finished size. A 10-foot-wide backdrop at 150 needs 18,000 px across if you take the number literally; in practice shops accept 100–150 on that width because the viewer is never close. Set the tag to 150 so preflight stops flagging a 72 export from Illustrator or a phone photo used as a panel.

Billboards and distance display. Highway boards, building wraps, and stadium graphics are specified even lower than 150 — often 10–50 DPI — because the viewing distance is measured in yards. 150 is already denser than those jobs need. Use it when the vendor wrote 150; do not “upgrade” a billboard file to 300. The extra pixels will not survive the print and will blow the file size.

150 vs 300 DPI

Same pixels, different claimed inches. That is the entire difference. A 3600 × 5400 px file tagged 150 prints 24 × 36 in; tagged 300 it prints 12 × 18 in. Neither tag adds detail. The one you pick is the one the downstream system asked to see — and, for large format, the one that matches how far away people will stand.

Changing the 150 DPI tag vs resampling the pixels

Those two operations are constantly sold as the same thing. They are not.

Metadata rewrite (this tool): the JPEG or PNG keeps every pixel sample. Only the density instruction changes. File size stays essentially identical. Quality is identical because the decoder never runs. Print size shrinks as DPI goes up, because the same pixels are asked to sit closer together.

Resampling (Photoshop Image Size with Resample on, most “enhance” websites): the software creates a new pixel grid. Going from 72 to 150 “the resample way” on a 72 DPI 24 × 36 in image tries to manufacture about 2.1× the pixels on each side. That is a new picture. It can look acceptable or it can look smeared; either way it is lossy, and for JPEG it is a second generation of compression on top.

Use the rewrite when the pixels already cover the print. Use a resample or an AI enlarger only when they do not. A quick test: divide width in pixels by 150. If the inches you get are at least the inches you will print, rewrite the tag and stop. If they are not, no DPI converter will save the job.

Frequently asked questions

When should I use 150 DPI?
Use 150 DPI for large-format printing viewed from a distance: posters, banners, trade-show graphics, and billboards. At arm’s length a photo lab still wants 300. A few feet back, the eye cannot resolve that extra density, so 150 is the usual spec — one quarter the pixels of 300 on the same paper (both dimensions halve: 24 × 36 in → 3600 × 5400 px at 150 versus 7200 × 10800 at 300), a much smaller file, and the RIP is happy.
Is 150 DPI good enough for printing?
Yes, when the piece is large and the viewer is not close. A 24 × 36 in poster, a retractable banner, or a booth graphic is typically specified at 150. A 4 × 6 in lab print, a passport photo, or an Etsy A4 printable still wants 300. This tool sets the tag you asked for. If the pixel count cannot support the physical size at 150, you need a larger original, not a higher tag.
What is the difference between 150 and 300 DPI?
Same pixels, different claimed inches. A 3600 × 5400 px file tagged 150 prints 24 × 36 in; tagged 300 it prints 12 × 18 in. Neither tag adds detail. 150 is for distance viewing (posters, banners, booths). 300 is the photo-lab and close-inspection habit. Screens ignore both tags.
How do I change my image to 150 DPI?
Drop a JPG or PNG into the tool on this page. The target is already 150. Click Download. Density metadata is rewritten in your browser — free, no signup, no watermark, no upload.
What print size will I get at 150 DPI?
Inches = pixels ÷ 150. A 1800 × 2400 px file prints 12 × 16 in; 2400 × 3000 px prints 16 × 20 in; 3600 × 5400 px prints 24 × 36 in — a standard poster. The tool shows the size for each file you drop.
Is 150 DPI OK for a poster?
Yes. 150 DPI is the usual floor for posters viewed from a few feet. A 24 × 36 in poster at 150 needs 3600 × 5400 px. 300 DPI on that same poster would demand 7200 × 10800 px and a much larger file for detail nobody can see from the aisle. If someone will inspect the poster at arm’s length, 300 is safer.
Does 150 DPI look blurry?
Not on a poster viewed from a few feet, and not on a screen at all. Screens display pixels, not the DPI tag. A 2000 × 1500 px image looks identical on a monitor whether it is tagged 72, 150, or 300. Up close on photo paper, 150 can look soft compared with 300. The tag only tells a printer how many inches those pixels should occupy.
Can I print a photo at 150 DPI?
You can, but it is the wrong number for a small photo. A 4 × 6 in lab print, a framed 8 × 10, or a photo book still wants 300. Use 150 for a large photographic poster or a booth graphic where the viewing distance is measured in feet, not inches. If the brief said 150, this page sets that tag losslessly.
