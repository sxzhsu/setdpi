Standard DPI Settings for Print, Web, and Scanning

A short list of integers — 72, 96, 150, 200, 300, 600, 1200 — that print shops, scanners, and upload forms keep asking for. Here is what each value is actually for.

The common DPI values, and what each is for

Standard DPI settings are not laws of physics. They are habits that escaped from 1980s monitors, photo labs, and press rooms and never left the forms. A JPEG or PNG stores one density pair — EXIF XResolution / YResolution, JFIF density, or PNG pHYs — and software labels that integer “DPI” or “PPI” depending on the decade. The useful question is which of these standards the job is asking for, and whether you have enough pixels to support the physical size at that number.

Print size in inches is always pixels ÷ DPI. Raising the tag shrinks the claimed inches. It does not add detail. Lowering it claims a larger print from the same samples. That arithmetic is the whole relationship. Everything else on this page is a map of which integer which desk expects to see.

DPIWhere it came fromWhat it is for
72Legacy Mac screen defaultEarly Macintosh displays were 72 pixels per inch so one screen pixel matched one PostScript point. The number became the default density tag in a lot of imaging software. It is not a print standard and not what modern screens use.
96Legacy Windows defaultEarly Windows displays were 96 PPI. Same story as 72: a leftover monitor default that still shows up when a tag is missing, or when an exporter writes a “screen” resolution. Screens ignore it either way.
150Viewing distanceLarge-format posters and banners that people stand several feet from. At that distance the eye does not resolve 300 samples per inch, so 150 is the usual spec — and a kinder pixel budget on an 24-inch-wide print.
200Newsprint and draftsNewspapers, some magazine ads on coarse stock, inkjet proofs, and digital archives. Sits between 150 (distance viewing) and 300 (photo-lab habit). Insertion orders often write this number on purpose.
300Photo-lab / magazine habitThe universal print standard: photo prints, magazines, business cards, journals, IDs, KDP interiors, Etsy printables. If nobody specified a number and you are printing a photograph, start here.
600Fine detailFine-art giclée, line art, small text, logos with hairlines, some medical and archival scans. Twice the samples of 300, which matters when the marks themselves are the content, not a photograph of a scene.
1200Press and archiveProfessional press work, precision labels, and archival scans of text or line art. This is capture or output addressability, not a photo-lab upload default. Do not tag a snapshot 1200 and expect a press to invent ink.

Two of those numbers — 72 and 96 — describe dead monitors. The other five describe jobs. Mixing them up is how a phone export tagged 72 gets bounced by a lab that only looked at the integer, and how a 600 DPI scan of a letter gets relabelled 72 on export and then looks “too small” in a layout program. Check the tag. Then decide whether the job wants that tag changed, or whether you actually need more pixels.

Which DPI does your job need?

Match the output, not a superstition. The same file can be correct at 150 on a poster and wrong at 150 on a 4 × 6 in lab print. Use this list, then confirm pixels ÷ chosen DPI ≥ the inches you will print.

  • Photo prints (4 × 6 through 8 × 10), magazines, business cards, flyers, DTG T-shirts. 300. This is the number photo labs, journal portals, and marketplace checklists look for. See DPI for printing for a pixels-needed table at 300.
  • Large posters, banners, trade-show graphics (18 × 24 and up). 150, sometimes 200. Viewers stand back. A 24 × 36 in poster at 300 would demand 7200 × 10800 px; at 150 it needs 3600 × 5400, which a decent camera can actually supply.
  • Newsprint ads, some magazine advertising, email proofs, office archives. 200. Coarser halftones cannot hold 300, and a lot of briefs write 200 on the insertion order. Use the 200 DPI preset when that is the number on the paper.
  • Canvas wraps. 150–200. The weave already breaks up fine detail; 300 is optional if you have the pixels, not required the way a hand-held print is.
  • Fine-art giclée of photographs. 300, or 600 if the shop asked for it and the file has the samples. Giclée printers can address more than 300; whether the photograph has more than 300 worth of information is a different question.
  • Line art, logos, small text, technical drawings. 600 or 1200. Photographs hide a coarse grid in grain and tone. Hairlines and 8-point type do not.
  • Scanning a print or a document. 200 for correspondence; 300 for photos you might reprint; 600 or 1200 for archives, line art, and small type. Scanner DPI actually creates pixels. Relabelling a 200 DPI scan as 600 does not.
  • Web, social, email, anything that stays on a screen. The tag does not matter. Pixel width matters. Browsers do not read JFIF. Write 300 only if a human or a form will inspect the integer later.

If two numbers could apply, pick the one written on the brief. If nothing is written and the piece will be held in the hand, 300 is the safer default. If nothing is written and the piece will be seen from a hallway, 150 is enough and kinder to the file.

Screens ignore the DPI tag

A 2000 × 1500 px image looks identical on a monitor whether it is tagged 72, 96, 200, or 300. Browsers map image pixels to CSS pixels. They do not consult the density field. That is why “save for web at 72 DPI” is a ritual, not a requirement, and why you cannot preview print sharpness by staring at the file on a laptop. For what 300 actually looks like on paper — a dot every 0.085 mm, below the eye at reading distance — see What 300 DPI looks like.

The tag starts to matter the moment a printer, a layout program, or a preflight script reads it. Those tools use it as a default placement size and as a pass/fail integer. A file with plenty of pixels and a 72 tag still fails a “minimum 300 DPI” checklist. That is a metadata problem, not a sharpness problem. Fix the tag. Do not resample unless the pixel count itself is short.

Frequently asked questions

What are standard DPI settings?
Standard DPI settings are the density values print, web, and scanning jobs keep asking for: 72 (legacy Mac screen), 96 (legacy Windows), 150 (large-format viewed from a distance), 200 (newsprint and some inkjet drafts), 300 (photo prints and magazines — the universal print standard), 600 (fine art, line art, small text), and 1200 (professional press and archival scans).
What is the standard DPI for printing?
300 DPI is the standard for photo prints, magazines, business cards, and most upload forms. Large posters and banners viewed from several feet away can use 150. Fine-art giclée and line art often want 600. Screens ignore the tag; printers and preflight software do not.
Is 72 DPI still a standard?
Only as a leftover. Early Macintosh displays were 72 pixels per inch, and that number became the default density tag in a lot of software. It is not a print standard and it is not what modern screens use. A file tagged 72 looks identical on a monitor to one tagged 300 with the same pixels.
Do I need 300 DPI for the web?
No. Browsers display pixels, not the DPI tag. A website, a social post, and an email image do not get sharper because you wrote 300 into the metadata. Use 300 when the file will be printed or when a portal checks the integer. For screen-only delivery, pixel dimensions matter; the tag does not.
How do I change a file to a standard DPI value?
Rewrite the density metadata. SetDPI does that in the browser without resampling or uploading: 150, 200, 300, 600, 1200, or any custom value on the homepage. Changing the tag does not add pixels. If the pixel count cannot support the print size at that DPI, you need a larger original.
What DPI should I scan at?
Match the job. Correspondence and office pages are often scanned at 200. Photo prints and documents you may reprint go to 300. Line art, small text, and archival work go to 600 or 1200. The scanner DPI is how many samples it takes per inch of the original — that actually creates pixels. Relabelling a 200 DPI scan as 600 does not.
